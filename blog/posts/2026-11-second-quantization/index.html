Second quantization for classical nonlinear dynamics

research
publications
A new paper in Nonlinearity uses the mathematics of many-particle quantum systems to build richer Koopman models of chaotic dynamics, with better prediction skill from the same eigenfunctions.
Published

November 18, 2026

A new paper by our group and colleagues at the Technische Universität Ilmenau, Second quantization for classical nonlinear dynamics, has been published in the journal Nonlinearity. It is open access. The paper takes a mathematical tool from many-body quantum physics and uses it to build more expressive models of how chaotic systems evolve. This post summarizes what the paper does, what the numerical results show, and how the work relates to the longer-term goals of the ESTCP project.

The research was carried out under other sources of support, which are listed in the paper. It belongs to the operator-theoretic foundation on which the project’s toolkit is built, described in the Methodology section, rather than to the project’s own demonstrations.

The problem: chaos and the Koopman operator

The forecasts on this site rest on the Koopman operator, which describes how quantities measured on a system, such as a temperature or a climate index, change over time. Its key advantage is that it is linear, even when the underlying dynamics are not. In practice the operator is approximated by a finite set of approximate eigenfunctions, each of which oscillates at its own frequency, and a forecast is built as a linear combination of them.

For chaotic systems this approach has a built-in limit. The Koopman operator of a mixing system has a continuous spectrum, so no finite collection of eigenfunctions can follow its evolution exactly. With a fixed number of eigenfunctions, the quality of a linear combination is capped, and the approximation can also produce values that are physically impossible, such as negative densities.

The idea: products of eigenfunctions

The paper enlarges the space in which the approximation is made. In addition to the eigenfunctions \(\zeta_j\) themselves, it uses their tensor products of all orders up to a chosen degree \(m\). A product of eigenfunctions again evolves as a pure oscillation, at the sum of their frequencies:

\[ \tilde U^t \big( \zeta_{j_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \zeta_{j_m} \big) = e^{i(\omega_{j_1} + \cdots + \omega_{j_m})\, t}\; \zeta_{j_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \zeta_{j_m}. \]

The natural setting for such products is a Fock space, the structure used in quantum field theory to describe systems with any number of particles; building it is known as second quantization. The paper introduces a new class of weighted Fock spaces that are also commutative algebras. It shows that their spectra split into tori, possibly of infinite dimension, on which the regularized Koopman evolution becomes a rotation. The construction is similar in spirit to a classical result of ergodic theory, the Halmos–von Neumann theorem, which represents systems with discrete spectra as rotations on tori.

In practical terms, a quantity to be forecast is represented as a polynomial of degree \(m\) on a \(d\)-dimensional torus, with coefficients computed from products of the eigenfunctions on the training data. The standard approach corresponds to \(m = 1\). The paper proves that the scheme converges to the true evolution in appropriate limits. For even \(m\) it also preserves positivity: a quantity that cannot be negative is never forecast as negative.

What the experiments show

The method is tested on two standard chaotic systems: a Stepanoff flow on a two-dimensional torus and the Lorenz 63 model. In both, 50 pairs of approximate Koopman eigenfunctions are used, for a standard approximation space of 101 functions. With degree \(m = 4\), the same eigenfunctions generate about 4.6 million coefficients.

For the Stepanoff flow, the quantity forecast is a smooth, strictly positive density with a single peak. The flow stretches and folds it into thin filaments, as shown in Figure 1. The standard approximation carries the S-shaped imprint of the eigenfunctions and takes negative values near the flow’s fixed point. The new approximation reproduces the round initial peak more closely and stays positive throughout.

Prediction skill is summarized in Figure 2 with the two measures used for the forecasts on this site, the normalized root-mean-square error and the anomaly correlation. For Lorenz 63, the Fock space approximation is better on both measures over the whole forecast interval tested, most clearly at short lead times; at the initial time its normalized error is below 0.1, against about 0.4 for the standard approximation. For the Stepanoff flow it is better up to about \(t = 2\) to \(3\), depending on the measure. At later times its error becomes slightly larger, concentrated in narrow bands where the true field has very sharp gradients.

All computations in the paper were run on conventional computers. In these tests the eigenfunctions were computed with the help of the known equations of motion of the two systems.

How this relates to the ESTCP project

The project’s forecasts, of ENSO, the MJO and weather at the Greenland station, are built from approximate Koopman eigenfunctions learned from observations. The paper suggests several ways in which these forecasts could be improved over the longer term.

The first is more skill from the same information. The Fock space construction draws on interactions between modes that a linear combination of eigenfunctions cannot represent. In climate terms, the product of an annual-cycle eigenfunction and an ENSO eigenfunction oscillates at a combination of their frequencies, which is one way in which the interplay between ENSO and the seasonal cycle could be captured.

The second is physical consistency. Many of the quantities that matter for hazards at installations cannot be negative, among them precipitation, wind speed and the probability of exceeding a threshold. A forecasting scheme that preserves positivity by construction avoids impossible values, which is of direct use when forecasts feed into decisions.

The third concerns computing. The method produces models with millions of coefficients estimated from large training sets, the kind of computation well suited to GPU hardware such as the project’s cluster. The paper also discusses implementations on quantum computers, which are the subject of ongoing work.

Applying the method to the project’s data will require further steps. Chief among them are computing the eigenfunctions from observations alone, without equations of motion, and testing the method at the lead times relevant to hazard forecasting.
