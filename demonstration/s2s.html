Weeks to months: subseasonal to seasonal

Where predictability comes from beyond two weeks, and why it matters for planning

The subseasonal to seasonal and interannual range: why it matters for DoW planning, the MJO and ENSO as sources of predictability, how they shift the odds of hazards in the demonstration regions, and what the demonstration forecasts at these lead times.
WarningDraft for internal review

Not yet cleared for public release.

Beyond about two weeks, day-to-day weather can no longer be forecast in detail: small errors in the starting state grow until a forecast is no better than climatology. Slowly evolving parts of the climate system still shift the odds of hazards weeks to months ahead. This subseasonal to seasonal range, from about two weeks to a season, and the interannual range beyond it, up to about two years, are where many planning decisions are made, and where forecast guidance is weakest.

Why it matters for planning

Forecasts in this range inform decisions such as:

  • scheduling exercises, training and maintenance around periods of likely heat, storms or poor visibility;
  • planning resupply, and pre-positioning supplies and equipment ahead of the typhoon or wildfire season, especially for remote islands;
  • preparing for drought, with its effects on water supply, dust and wildfire risk;
  • seasonal readiness at installations.

Sources of predictability

The Madden–Julian Oscillation

The MJO is a large-scale pattern of enhanced and suppressed tropical rainfall that moves eastward from the Indian Ocean into the Pacific, recurring every 30 to 90 days or so (Madden and Julian 1971; Zhang 2005).

  • Western Pacific. Active phases bring bursts of storminess and favor the formation of typhoons; suppressed phases bring short dry spells.
  • Southwestern United States. The MJO modulates surges of moist air from the Gulf of California into northwestern Mexico and Arizona, and with them the timing of active and break periods of the North American Monsoon (Lorenz and Hartmann 2006).
  • In the demonstration. The MJO is tracked in daily ERA5 data, and its forecasts target outgoing longwave radiation over the western Pacific.

The El Niño–Southern Oscillation

ENSO is a coupled oscillation of the tropical Pacific Ocean and atmosphere, with warm El Niño and cool La Niña phases every two to seven years (Trenberth 1997).

  • Western Pacific. ENSO shifts where typhoons form and how strong they become (Wang and Chan 2002). In Micronesia and the Marshall Islands, drought often follows an El Niño, and sea level falls during El Niño and rises during La Niña.
  • Southwestern United States. El Niño winters tend to be wetter than normal and La Niña winters drier, and both phases change the odds of floods and droughts (Cayan et al. 1999).
  • In the demonstration. ENSO is tracked in monthly ERA5 data, and its forecasts target the Niño 3.4 sea surface temperature index.

The annual cycle and long-term trends

The toolkit separates the annual cycle and the long-term warming trend from ENSO in sea surface temperature, so that each can be tracked on its own (Froyland et al. 2021). This matters for planning, because a hazard that is rare today may become more frequent as the climate warms.

How the modes shift the odds of hazards

Region MJO ENSO
Southwestern United States Timing of moisture surges and of active and break periods of the summer monsoon Wetter El Niño winters and drier La Niña winters; changed odds of floods and droughts
Tropical western Pacific Bursts of storminess and typhoon formation in active phases; dry spells in suppressed phases Where typhoons form and how strong they become; drought after El Niño; changes in sea level

How good can these forecasts be?

  • Useful but limited. Skill in this range varies strongly with region, season and the state of the modes (Vitart et al. 2017). Forecasts made when the MJO or ENSO is strong are usually more skillful than forecasts made when they are weak.
  • The MJO. In the operational forecast systems of the international S2S project, MJO skill extends to a few weeks, depending on the model (Vitart et al. 2017).
  • ENSO. Real-time ENSO forecasts from many models have been evaluated over 2002–2011; forecasts made in the northern spring, across the so-called spring predictability barrier, are the least skillful (Barnston et al. 2012).
  • A real-time test. The tropical Pacific was developing an El Niño in 2026, possibly a strong one. The real-time season of 2026/27 follows it as a case study, a demanding test of the ENSO forecasts and of their links to hazards at the stations.
