The problem

Weather and climate hazards at DoW installations, and the gap between weather forecasts and climate projections

The problem the demonstration addresses: the hazards that affect DoW installations, the forecast horizons that planning needs, the gap between weather and climate prediction, and what the demonstration tests.
WarningDraft for internal review

This section summarizes the project’s Demonstration Plan and draft final report, which have not yet been cleared for public release. Review it with the project leads, and the station information with the data provider, before making the website public.

Hazards at installations

Extreme heat, drought, flash floods, dust, tropical cyclones and Arctic cold disrupt flight operations, damage infrastructure, degrade sensors, and delay the movement of people and supplies at installations in the continental United States and overseas. The same hazards matter on very different timescales. A thunderstorm or a fog bank decides whether an airfield can operate this afternoon; a developing El Niño changes the odds of drought or typhoons for the coming season.

The gap between weather and climate

Planning for these hazards draws on forecasts at very different horizons:

  • Weather forecasts from numerical weather prediction support decisions over the next several days.
  • Climate projections support decisions about infrastructure over decades.
  • In between lies the range from about two weeks to a few years, the weather-to-climate continuum. Forecasts in this range inform the scheduling of exercises, the pre-positioning of supplies, seasonal readiness and maintenance planning, yet it is where forecast guidance is weakest.

Skill beyond two weeks comes mostly from slowly evolving parts of the climate system. The most important are the Madden–Julian Oscillation (MJO), which varies over about 30 to 90 days (Madden and Julian 1971; Zhang 2005), and the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO), which varies over about two to seven years (Trenberth 1997). Both affect hazards across the Indo-Pacific and North America. International experiments show that forecasts in this range have useful but limited skill, which varies strongly with region, season and the state of these modes (Vitart et al. 2017).

The two timescales have their own pages: hours to days, and weeks to months.

Why a data-driven toolkit

Physics-based forecast systems are the backbone of operational prediction, but they have drawbacks in this range and at the scale of a single installation:

  • They are expensive to run and to start from observations.
  • They must approximate processes they cannot resolve, which biases their simulation of intermittent modes such as the MJO.
  • Their grids are coarse compared with a single installation.
  • In data-sparse regions, such as the Arctic, few observations constrain them.

Simple statistical forecasts, such as persistence, climatology or autoregressive models, are cheap, but they miss the nonlinear, multiscale structure of the climate system and are usually limited to short lead times or to a single mode.

The project’s toolkit sits between the two. It learns the dynamics from data using Koopman operator theory, extracts indices of recurring phenomena such as ENSO and the MJO, and uses them to make forecasts with quantified uncertainty (see Methodology). The methods were validated in peer-reviewed studies with a research code written in MATLAB. The project turns that code into an open-source Python toolkit that runs on ordinary computers and on GPUs, and that analysts who are not specialists can use.

Working with the 14th Weather Squadron

The U.S. Air Force 14th Weather Squadron holds long-term climate records for DoW-relevant stations. Through the project, the team obtained daily records from about 140 stations in the southwestern United States, the tropical western Pacific and Greenland, most of them from 1975 to the present. They make a realistic test, in which the toolkit is compared with the simple baselines that forecasters already use. Discussions with the squadron have guided the choice of test cases. One result is the addition of an Arctic station, where forecasts a few days ahead are of high interest and numerical guidance is limited.

What the demonstration tests

The demonstration is built in four layers, each answering a question a prospective user would ask. Each layer feeds the next.

  1. Is the software correct, fast and portable? The toolkit is first tested on the Lorenz 63 system, whose dynamics, spectrum and forecast error growth are known independently, and whose size can be scaled up freely.
  2. Does it find the recognized modes of variability, and can it forecast them? ENSO and the MJO are extracted from ERA5 monthly and ERA5 daily data, where decades of records and conventional indices are available for comparison.
  3. Does it forecast conditions at the stations, and are the forecasts reliable? Daily records from stations in the southwestern United States, the tropical western Pacific and Greenland are forecast with station data alone and with added reanalysis and Koopman-index predictors.
  4. Can analysts use the results? Structured sessions with prospective users evaluate usability, interpretability and relevance.

The Koopman indices verified in the second layer become predictors in the third. The targets and lead times of each layer are listed under forecast targets.

Policy context

The demonstration does not respond to a regulatory requirement, and it involves no field work and no change to any installation. It supports DoW policy on hazard-informed planning:

  • DoD Instruction 3020.45, Mission Assurance Construct, calls for maintaining mission-essential functions in the presence of environmental disruption.
  • DoD Instruction 4715.03, Natural Resources Conservation Program, calls for environmental data to be used in installation planning.
  • The Unified Facilities Criteria for the design and operation of installations build in resilience considerations that depend on reliable information about hazards.
  • The Air Force, Army and Navy installation resilience and adaptation plans use information on the weeks-to-years range.
