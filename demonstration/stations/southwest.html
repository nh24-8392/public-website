Southwestern United States

The primary demonstration region: 96 stations and eight anchor sites

The southwestern station network of the demonstration: its landscapes, climate and hazards, the eight anchor sites, profiles of every station, and the planned results.
WarningDraft for internal review

The southwestern network is the main test of the station forecasts. It has 96 stations across southern California, Arizona, New Mexico, west Texas and northern Mexico, and eight anchor sites where the forecasts are evaluated in detail. How the forecasts are made and judged is described in the experiment design.

Setting

The network reaches from the Pacific coast of California to west Texas, nearly 2,000 km to the east, and south into seven Mexican states. The region is arid to semi-arid. Elevations range from about 40 ft below sea level in the Imperial Valley to 6,814 ft at Sierra Blanca Regional Airport in New Mexico.

Weather hazards through the year

  • Winter storms (about December to March). Pacific storms, including atmospheric rivers, bring most of the year’s rain to the California coast and mountains, and can cause flash floods and debris-laden runoff in canyon-fed basins. Freezing rain and ice occasionally affect the High Plains.
  • Fog and low cloud. The southern California coast has frequent marine-layer fog and low stratus, especially in late spring and summer. Inland valleys, such as the Central Valley, have persistent radiation fog in winter.
  • Wind and dust (spring). Strong frontal passages raise dust over the High Plains and the desert basins.
  • Dryline storms (spring and early summer). Thunderstorms with hail and tornadoes form along the dryline in eastern New Mexico and west Texas.
  • Extreme heat (summer). Most severe in the low desert basins, where temperatures often exceed 110°F (43°C). Heat reduces aircraft performance and stresses people and equipment.
  • North American Monsoon (about July to September). Subtropical moisture brings daily thunderstorms across Arizona, New Mexico, southern California, Nevada and northwestern Mexico, with heavy local rain, frequent lightning, intense downbursts and flash floods. Dust-laden outflows can form towering haboobs with near-zero visibility, especially early in the season.
  • Santa Ana winds and wildfire (fall and early winter). Dry downslope winds that can exceed 60–80 mph drive large wildfires, with smoke that spreads hundreds of miles.

Year-to-year variability and large-scale influences

Monsoon strength, onset and duration, the intensity of the wildfire season, and multi-year droughts all vary strongly from year to year. Drought also makes dust events more frequent.

  • ENSO is a principal source of year-to-year variability in winter precipitation. El Niño winters tend to be wetter than normal across much of the region and La Niña winters drier, and both phases change the odds of floods and droughts (Cayan et al. 1999).
  • The MJO modulates surges of moist air from the Gulf of California into northwestern Mexico and Arizona, and with them the timing of active and break periods of the monsoon (Lorenz and Hartmann 2006).

These are the modes that the Koopman ENSO and MJO indices track. The station forecasts test whether adding the indices as predictors improves skill at the stations.

Eight anchor sites were selected for the diversity of their terrain and hazards. The detailed station-level evaluation is reported for these sites, while the full network is used to learn regional patterns and to compute aggregate statistics. They are the red diamonds on the map in Regional characteristics.

KEDW · Edwards Air Force Base, CA

In the western Mojave Desert, among the dry Rogers and Rosamond lakebeds. The basin has large day–night temperature swings, frequent downslope winds and little cloud. Visibility is usually good, but temperature and turbulence near the ground are hard to forecast, and occasional thunderstorms start along outflow from the Sierra Nevada. Crosswind shear, dew-point depression and density altitude are key forecast quantities.

  • Short-term hazards: Winds loft fine dust from Rogers Dry Lake, reducing visibility and wearing engines. Strong thermal turbulence and dust devils on hot afternoons. Occasional frontal winds above 40–50 mph, and rare rain that floods the lakebeds.
  • Long-term risks: Large day–night temperature swings stress runways and structures. Changing wind patterns could affect planning for flights and landings, and persistent dust requires constant mitigation.

KNJK · Naval Air Facility El Centro (Vraciu Field), CA

In the Imperial Valley, a desert basin about 42 ft below sea level. Strong surface heating, with summer temperatures that routinely exceed 110°F, raises the density altitude and produces low-level turbulence and convective updrafts. Moisture from the nearby Salton Sea adds variability in humidity that can degrade optical and infrared sensors.

  • Short-term hazards: In the Imperial Valley about 42 ft below sea level: summer temperatures often above 115°F, low-level thermal turbulence, and frequent dust from desert winds and farming.
  • Long-term risks: Persistent drought, airborne dust and surface erosion degrade equipment and infrastructure. The isolated location makes resupply more sensitive to disruption.

KVBG · Vandenberg Space Force Base, CA

On rugged coastal bluffs of California’s Central Coast, where cold ocean currents and a coastal eddy create sharp microclimates: sudden onset of fog, low stratus and temperature inversions. The base is exposed to fire weather during Santa Ana wind events and, over the long term, to coastal erosion.

  • Short-term hazards: Where the Pacific coast meets the Transverse Ranges: highly vulnerable to wildfires driven by terrain-channeled winds and dry vegetation, such as the 2016 Canyon Fire, which burned more than 12,000 acres. Persistent marine layers and coastal fog frequently impair visual and infrared tracking.
  • Long-term risks: Prolonged drought, salt-air corrosion and coastal erosion. Shifting fog bands and temperature inversions are hard to model, and rugged terrain with limited evacuation routes complicates wildfire response.

KNSI · San Nicolas Island Naval Outlying Field, CA

A remote island airfield about 55 nautical miles offshore of Point Mugu, in a zone of persistent marine stratus. Low cloud ceilings, coastal fog and strong crosswinds are frequent. The island is isolated, with no nearby alternate airfield, so accurate short-term forecasts matter, and constant wind and salt air speed up equipment wear.

  • Short-term hazards: An exposed island with little shelter from terrain: strong onshore winds, frequent low marine stratus and salt-laden fog degrade visibility and radio signal quality.
  • Long-term risks: Constant corrosive marine air degrades exposed equipment. Rough seas or prolonged fog can delay resupply, and erosion of the shallow mesas and windblown sand can undermine foundations.

KDMA · Davis–Monthan Air Force Base (Tucson), AZ

In a low basin of the Sonoran Desert at Tucson, framed by the Santa Catalina and Rincon Mountains. Spring and summer temperatures are among the highest in the country, and convective dust storms are frequent in the monsoon season. Air forced up the surrounding mountains often triggers fast-developing thunderstorms with a risk of microbursts, and the city’s heat island adds to the heat. Wind shear, the mixing of dust layers and fire weather are key forecast quantities.

  • Short-term hazards: Monsoon thunderstorms with flash floods and lightning. Convective dust storms (haboobs) roll through Tucson. Occasional microburst wind shear threatens parked aircraft and shade structures.
  • Long-term risks: Long runs of 100°F+ days force adjusted maintenance schedules and raise cooling demand. Wildfire smoke from nearby mountains lowers summer air quality, and water scarcity is a concern.

KCVS · Cannon Air Force Base (Clovis), NM

On the High Plains of eastern New Mexico, among rolling mesas and farmland that produce seasonal blowing dust and high pollen counts. In spring the position of the dryline varies from day to day, so clear skies can give way quickly to thunderstorms with hail. Sudden drops in cloud ceiling, dew-point spread and low-level turbulence are key forecast quantities.

  • Short-term hazards: Sudden dryline thunderstorms near the New Mexico–Texas line, with downbursts, hail and tornadoes. Frequent wind shear and dust lofting over the open High Plains. Gusty low-level winds.
  • Long-term risks: Periodic drought intensifies dust and reduces low-level moisture, at times obscuring night skies. Extreme heat stresses engines and electronic cooling, and large temperature swings wear runways and hangars.

KABQ · Albuquerque International Sunport (Kirtland AFB), NM

In the Rio Grande valley below the Sandia Mountains, next to Kirtland AFB. The mountains generate mountain waves and gravity waves and make the vertical temperature profile variable. Dryline convection from the southeast brings dust and the threat of supercell thunderstorms. Elevated mixed layers and strong daytime heating complicate forecasts.

  • Short-term hazards: Summer monsoon thunderstorms with lightning, microbursts and flash floods in arroyos. Occasional dust storms (haboobs) reduce visibility.
  • Long-term risks: Extreme heat strains roads and runways. Drought and water scarcity affect utilities and firefighting resources. Inversion layers trap pollutants.

KLBB · Lubbock Preston Smith International Airport, TX

On the southern High Plains, one of the most volatile weather regions of the United States. Fast-developing thunderstorms, dryline interactions and dust from the surrounding farmland are frequent, bringing hail, wind shear and, in season, tornadoes. In winter, occasional freezing rain and ice affect the airfield.

  • Short-term hazards: In the southern Plains’ Tornado Alley: spring severe thunderstorms with large hail and tornado risk. High Plains winds raise dust storms, such as the well-known 2011 Lubbock haboob. Occasional winter ice storms coat runways.
  • Long-term risks: Drought cycles intensify dust and sandblasting. Swings from 100°F summers to winter snow complicate maintenance, and nearby wind farms may slightly alter low-level winds.

KLPC · Lompoc Airport

KMYF · Montgomery–Gibbs Executive Airport (San Diego)

KSMO · Santa Monica Municipal Airport

  • Long-term risks: Prolonged drought raises fire risk, heat extremes stress infrastructure and electronics, and atmospheric rivers bring a potential for flooding.

KTOA · Zamperini Field (Torrance)

KTRM · Jacqueline Cochran Regional Airport

  • Short-term hazards: Intense summer heat, often 115–120°F, limits aircraft performance. Frequent dust storms and gusty crosswinds reduce visibility, especially in the monsoon season.
  • Long-term risks: Persistent drought and stressed vegetation raise wildfire risk. Heat accelerates wear on runways and equipment.

KVBG · Vandenberg Space Force Base · anchor site

  • Short-term hazards: Where the Pacific coast meets the Transverse Ranges: highly vulnerable to wildfires driven by terrain-channeled winds and dry vegetation, such as the 2016 Canyon Fire, which burned more than 12,000 acres. KVNY · Van Nuys Airport

KWJF · General William J. Fox Airfield

  • Short-term hazards: Strong westerly winds and blowing dust in the Antelope Valley, especially in the afternoon, impair visibility for helicopters and low-flying aircraft.
  • Long-term risks: The semi-arid climate and recurring drought raise wildfire risk along access routes. Temperature extremes wear avionics and ground-based radar.

Brown Field Municipal Airport, San Diego

  • Short-term hazards: Near the U.S.–Mexico border: strong crosswinds, seasonal dust incursions and heat-driven changes in density altitude.
  • Long-term risks: Wildfire encroachment and prolonged heat stress navigation equipment and infrastructure. Regional haze and pollution degrade visibility.

  • Short-term hazards: Gusty afternoon winds, sudden dust intrusions and summer monsoon storms reduce visibility.
  • Long-term risks: Fire seasons and prolonged drought raise wildfire risk in the surrounding terrain. Strong ultraviolet exposure, thermal fatigue and monsoon flash floods shorten the life of infrastructure.

KDVT · Phoenix Deer Valley Airport

  • Short-term hazards: In the northern Phoenix basin: frequent convective turbulence, strong thermal gradients and summer dust intrusions.
  • Long-term risks: Phoenix’s urban heat island and summer temperatures often above 115°F (46°C) stress avionics and airfield surfaces. Persistent drought and windborne dust accelerate wear.

KFFZ · Falcon Field Airport (Mesa)

  • Short-term hazards: In the Phoenix metro area of the Sonoran Desert: high summer heat, gusty surface winds, and reduced visibility from blowing dust and occasional wildfire smoke. Thermal turbulence and high density altitude, especially in the afternoon.
  • Long-term risks: Extreme heat, ultraviolet light and dust wear infrastructure, electronics and runway surfaces, and the urban heat island adds to the stress.

KFHU · Libby Army Airfield (Fort Huachuca)

  • Short-term hazards: Sudden summer monsoon storms with lightning, downbursts and dust. Moderate turbulence from the nearby Huachuca Mountains.
  • Long-term risks: Persistent drought raises wildfire risk in adjacent grasslands and scrub. Extreme temperatures strain runways and electronic equipment, and older infrastructure needs constant maintenance.

KGXF · Gila Bend Air Force Auxiliary Field

  • Short-term hazards: Seasonal monsoon storms with convective turbulence, microbursts and flash flooding. Summer heat regularly exceeds 110°F, reducing lift and stressing aircraft systems.
  • Long-term risks: Persistent aridity, extreme temperature cycles and dust wear runways and avionics. Periodic wildfires in the surrounding areas reduce visibility and access.

KGYR · Phoenix Goodyear Airport

  • Short-term hazards: In the Sonoran Desert west of Phoenix: extreme heat, high density altitude and seasonal dust intrusions that reduce visibility. Shared airspace with nearby Luke AFB.
  • Long-term risks: Ultraviolet light, temperature extremes and dust wear airfield infrastructure and avionics. Intermittent high winds and wildfire smoke from the surrounding desert and mountains.

KIWA · Phoenix–Mesa Gateway Airport (former Williams AFB)

  • Short-term hazards: Monsoon storms bring sudden microburst winds and localized flooding on the flat airfield. Summer dust storms from desert and farmland reduce visibility. Occasional fog on winter mornings.
  • Long-term risks: Summer heat, often above 110°F, affects infrastructure and can restrict flights through high density altitude. Ozone and dust degrade air quality, and water availability is a concern as Phoenix grows.

KLUF · Luke Air Force Base

  • Short-term hazards: High-intensity dust storms (haboobs) from the Sonoran Desert west of Phoenix cause sudden loss of visibility and airborne particulates. Monsoon storms from July to September bring convective bursts, lightning and runway washout risk.
  • Long-term risks: Persistent drought and temperatures above 115°F stress avionics, cooling systems and airfield power. Strain on the regional power grid is a further concern.

KNYL · Marine Corps Air Station Yuma

  • Short-term hazards: Arid climate with extreme heat, often above 100°F (38°C), affecting people and equipment. Dust storms reduce visibility.
  • Long-term risks: Prolonged drought raises wildfire risk in the surrounding desert, and water scarcity affects long-term sustainability.

KPHX · Phoenix Sky Harbor International Airport

  • Short-term hazards: Summer heat of 110°F+ reduces aircraft lift and engine performance, and high density altitude narrows takeoff and landing margins. Nearby terrain and urban thermal gradients increase turbulence.
  • Long-term risks: Regional drought, poor air quality and wildfires threaten infrastructure and visibility.

KPRC · Prescott Regional Airport

  • Short-term hazards: Gusty, variable afternoon southwest winds over the high-desert terrain. Localized turbulence and thermals in the warm season.
  • Long-term risks: Increasing wildfire exposure and heat events threaten access, degrade sensors and cause long periods of poor air quality. Dryness and dust raise maintenance needs.

KSAD · Safford Regional Airport

  • Short-term hazards: Strong westerly winds and seasonal dust storms rapidly reduce visibility. Summer monsoon weather brings convective turbulence, lightning and brief intense rain.
  • Long-term risks: The semi-arid location is increasingly vulnerable to wildfires and heat damage to airfield surfaces and navigation systems. Long dry periods increase wind erosion and dust.

KSDL · Scottsdale Airport

  • Short-term hazards: In the northeastern Phoenix metro area: intense afternoon thermals, convective turbulence and dust events reduce visibility in the hot months.
  • Long-term risks: The Sonoran Desert climate of extreme heat, prolonged drought and strong ultraviolet light wears infrastructure and electronics. Urban growth limits room for expansion.

KTUS · Tucson International Airport

  • Short-term hazards: Intense summer heat, dry convective winds and frequent dust intrusions complicate approaches and reduce visibility. Approach corridors overlap with nearby Davis–Monthan AFB.
  • Long-term risks: Heat, ultraviolet exposure and dust abrasion wear runways and airfield systems. Seasonal wildfires, intensified by drought, can disrupt flights and access.

KHOB · Lea County Regional Airport

KLRU · Las Cruces International Airport

  • Short-term hazards: Frequent dust events as westerly winds carry sand from White Sands. Thunderstorm downbursts and dry lightning over the Organ Mountains. Occasional dense fog in the Rio Grande valley in the cooler months.

KROW · Roswell International Air Center

KSRR · Sierra Blanca Regional Airport

KTCS · Truth or Consequences Municipal Airport
