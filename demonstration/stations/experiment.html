Experiment design

How the station forecasts are made and judged

Design of the station forecast experiment: the station records, processing and quality control, predictors and lead times, training and test periods, baselines, verification, hazard statistics, event case studies and the real-time season.
WarningDraft for internal review

Not yet cleared for public release. This page follows the Demonstration Plan and the draft final report. Details, such as the training and test years, will be fixed in the Final Demonstration Plan.

The station experiment asks whether the toolkit can forecast conditions at the stations, and whether its forecasts are reliable. It is the third layer of the demonstration: the Koopman indices of ENSO and the MJO, extracted from ERA5 monthly and ERA5 daily data, become predictors for the station forecasts.

The station records

The records are daily statistics supplied by the U.S. Air Force 14th Weather Squadron, for about 140 stations in the southwestern United States and northern Mexico, the tropical western Pacific, and Greenland. Most records start in 1975 and run to the present. The record at Guam spans more than 70 years.

Variable Daily statistics
Temperature Maximum, minimum and mean
Sea-level pressure Maximum, minimum and mean
Station pressure Maximum, minimum and mean
Wind Mean zonal (east–west) and meridional (north–south) components
Precipitation Daily total

The raw records are used under the terms of the data transfer and are not redistributed. The open-source toolkit contains the code that reads them, but not the records themselves.

Processing and quality control

  1. Common format. Each station’s records are converted to a uniform daily format, and units are harmonized, for example pressure in hectopascals.
  2. Continuous time axis. The dates are completed to a continuous daily axis. Missing days are filled by linear interpolation, and the number of filled days is reported for each station and variable.
  3. Screening. Duplicate and invalid entries are removed. Each record is compared with the nearest ERA5 grid cell to flag outliers, unit errors and breaks in the series.
  4. Anomalies. A climatology for each calendar day, estimated over the training period, is removed, and variables can be standardized.
  5. Gap tests. Days and blocks of days, from one day to several weeks, are removed at random from complete records, such as the long Guam record, and filled again, to measure how much the interpolation affects the results. Skill is also recomputed with interpolated days left out of the verification.

Each station has an entry in a metadata table, with its identifier, name, coordinates, elevation where available, data source, variables, period of record and fraction of missing days. The processing history of each input is stored with the results.

Predictors, targets and lead times

Three sets of predictors are compared:

  1. Station only: the station’s own variables.
  2. Station plus ERA5: with large-scale ERA5 fields added.
  3. Station plus Koopman indices: with the MJO and ENSO indices added.

Forecasts are made with kernel analog forecasting. Forecast probabilities come from the kernel weights of the analog forecasts. Probabilistic forecasts with quantum mechanical data assimilation are planned: QMDA is implemented in the MATLAB version of the toolkit but not yet in the Python version (see the Documentation).

Target Sampling Lead times
Station temperature, pressure and wind anomalies Daily 1–14 days
Station weekly and monthly means Weekly, monthly 1–4 weeks; 1–3 months

Anomalies are departures from the climatology of the calendar day. Weekly and monthly means are used at the longer leads, where daily values are no longer predictable. The full network of each region is used to learn regional patterns and to compute aggregate statistics; the anchor and forward sites are evaluated in detail.

Training and test periods

  • Chronological split. The training period comes before the test period. Any training sample whose delay window, or the stretch of days it is asked to predict, overlaps the test period is excluded, so no forecast uses information from the period it is verified against.
  • Climatology from training data. The climatologies that define the anomalies are estimated on the training period only.
  • Long test periods. Test periods are several years long and include unusual seasons, so that skill is not judged on a single regime.
  • A forecast every day. Forecasts are started on every day of the test period.
  • How much data is needed. The training record is also shortened, for example 1975–2000 instead of 1975–2015, and skill is computed again.

Baselines

Every forecast is scored against the same simple baselines, computed on the same days and anomalies. Their purpose is to find the lead time beyond which the toolkit adds information, not to stand in for the best operational forecasts.

Baseline Definition
Persistence Today’s anomaly, carried forward unchanged to every lead time
Climatology The long-term average for the calendar day, that is, a zero anomaly
Autoregressive models, AR(1) to AR(3) A linear regression of the anomaly on its last one to three values, fitted on the training period
Station-only toolkit The toolkit with predictors limited to the station’s own variables, which isolates the benefit of the added predictors

Scores

For a forecast \(\hat a_n(\tau)\) issued on day \(n\) for lead time \(\tau\), and the anomaly \(a_{n+\tau}\) that actually occurred, the anomaly correlation and the normalized root-mean-square error over the test period are

\[ \mathrm{AC}(\tau) = \frac{\sum_n \left(a_{n+\tau} - \bar a_\tau\right)\left(\hat a_n(\tau) - \bar{\hat a}_\tau\right)} {\left[\sum_n \left(a_{n+\tau} - \bar a_\tau\right)^2 \, \sum_n \left(\hat a_n(\tau) - \bar{\hat a}_\tau\right)^2\right]^{1/2}}, \qquad \mathrm{NRMSE}(\tau) = \left[\frac{\sum_n \left(a_{n+\tau} - \hat a_n(\tau)\right)^2}{\sum_n a_{n+\tau}^2}\right]^{1/2}, \]

where the overbars are means over the test period. An NRMSE of 1 is the error of the climatology forecast. Skill relative to a baseline is reported as the mean-square-error skill score, \(1 - \mathrm{NRMSE}^2 / \mathrm{NRMSE}_\mathrm{ref}^2\), and the bias is the mean forecast error.

Probabilistic forecasts are scored with the continuous ranked probability score and with Brier scores for exceeding the 10th and 90th percentiles of the climatological distribution (Gneiting and Raftery 2007). Reliability diagrams and rank histograms show whether the forecast probabilities can be taken at face value (Hamill 2001), and the spread–skill ratio compares the spread of the forecast ensemble with its error.

Statistical significance. Successive daily forecasts are far from independent, so naive confidence intervals would overstate significance. The uncertainty of every score is estimated with a moving-block bootstrap, using blocks longer than the time over which anomalies stay correlated. A forecast counts as an improvement on a baseline only if the confidence interval of the difference excludes zero. Skill is shown as a function of lead time with its confidence band, so the lead at which the toolkit stops improving on a baseline can be read off the plot. Scores aggregated over stations weight every station equally, and their spread across stations is shown as well.

Success criteria

Objective Success criterion
Station-level skill Anomaly correlation and NRMSE better than all three baselines at leads of 1–5 days at a majority of the anchor sites; an anomaly correlation of at least 0.5 at a lead of 3 days at Pituffik; added predictors extend skill beyond the station-only forecasts
Hazard statistics and case studies Station conditions given the phase of an index differ significantly from climatology, and more so for the Koopman indices than for conventional ones; these conditional statistics can be predicted weeks to months ahead; in each case study, the forecast probability of the event exceeds its climatological rate at a useful lead time
Uncertainty Rank histograms close to flat; spread–skill ratio near one; reliability within sampling uncertainty; continuous ranked probability score better than that of climatology

Hazard statistics

The Koopman indices are useful for hazards if they tell us how the odds of a hazard change, and if those odds can themselves be forecast.

  • Conditional distributions. A station variable, such as daily precipitation or maximum temperature, is filtered to keep the intraseasonal or the interannual band. Its distribution is then computed separately for each phase of the index and compared with the overall climatological distribution. Significance is tested by shuffling the index in blocks.
  • Koopman against conventional indices. The same comparison is made with conventional indices: extended empirical orthogonal functions, the real-time multivariate MJO index and the Niño 3.4 index.
  • Hazards as local extremes. A hazard is defined as exceeding a percentile of the station’s own climatology, for example the 95th percentile of daily maximum temperature, so that the event has the same climatological frequency at every station.
  • Forecast odds. Probabilities of exceeding the threshold, conditioned on the forecast index, are verified with Brier scores and reliability diagrams.

Event case studies

Three case studies test whether the toolkit would have given useful warning of specific events.

Event Question Status
E1. The 2026/27 El Niño and its effects on the demonstration regions How early and how accurately did the toolkit foresee the strength and timing of the event, and how much does the ENSO index say about winter conditions at the stations? Selected; in progress
E2. A hazard event in the southwestern United States Do forecasts of the indices raise the probability of a heat, monsoon, dust or wildfire-weather event above climatology at a useful lead time? To be selected
E3. A hazard event in the western Pacific or at Pituffik Does the toolkit foresee a burst of convection, a typhoon passage, or a cold or wind event at a useful lead time? To be selected

Each study reports the state of the relevant index at and before the event; the forecasts of the index and of the event probability at each lead time, compared with the climatological rate and the baselines; the conditional statistics of the station variables; and a short, general summary of the event. A missed event is reported as prominently as a success.

To avoid choosing events after the fact because the toolkit did well on them, events must meet criteria set in advance:

  • a documented impact, in the records of the 14th Weather Squadron or in public sources;
  • continuous data at the nearest stations around the event;
  • for past events, the event and a buffer as long as the longest lead time are left out of training;
  • a link to a mode that the toolkit tracks;
  • a spread of regions and hazards across the three studies;
  • agreement of the 14th Weather Squadron.

The real-time season

Alongside the retrospective forecasts, a real-time season is proposed for November 2026 to April 2027. Five rules keep it a fair test:

  1. Frozen configuration. All settings are chosen with data from before the season, recorded with a version tag, and not changed during the season. Retraining with new data is allowed, with the same settings.
  2. Past data only. A forecast issued on a given day uses only observations available on that day.
  3. A fixed schedule. Station forecasts are issued daily, MJO outlooks at least every five days, and ENSO forecasts monthly.
  4. Archived before verification. Each forecast is stored with its issue date, configuration tag and a checksum, and posted to the forecast archive, so it cannot be changed once the outcome is known.
  5. Same verification as the hindcasts. The same scores, data and baselines are used, for the season as a whole and, for the stations, for the months in which the hazards of interest occur.
