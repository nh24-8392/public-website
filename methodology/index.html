Methodology

The operator-theoretic framework underlying the analyses and forecasts

Overview of the operator-theoretic framework behind the analyses and forecasts: Koopman operators, kernel methods, kernel analog forecasting and quantum mechanical data assimilation.

The analyses and forecasts presented on this website are obtained with a data-driven framework in which the Koopman operator formalism of ergodic theory is combined with kernel methods from machine learning. The equations of motion of the atmosphere and ocean are not integrated. Instead, the action of the dynamics on observables, such as a sea surface temperature field or the daily temperature recorded at a station, is estimated from long time series of observations (Berry et al. 2020; Froyland et al. 2021).

NoteScope of this section

The pages in this section summarize the mathematical basis of the toolkit. Most of the material is earlier work. It was developed over more than a decade by a number of authors and collaborators, under other sources of support, and it was not carried out as part of the present project unless this is stated. The original publications are cited throughout, and the sources of support are listed under Resources. The work of the project itself is presented under Demonstration and Documentation.

Outline of the approach

Let \(\Phi^t\) denote the flow of the system on its state space \(X\), and let \(\mu\) be an invariant probability measure. The Koopman operator \(U^t\) acts on observables \(f \in L^2(\mu)\) by composition with the flow, \(U^t f = f \circ \Phi^t\). It is a linear operator, although \(\Phi^t\) is in general nonlinear. Two uses are made of it. Its eigenfunctions, or approximate eigenfunctions when the spectrum is continuous, evolve periodically along trajectories, and they serve as indices of recurrent phenomena such as the annual cycle, the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) and the Madden–Julian Oscillation (MJO). Its action on a given observable, or the action of its adjoint on probability densities, yields forecasts together with measures of their uncertainty.

The approximation of \(U^t\) from data proceeds as follows. The input is a time series of observations, which may consist of station records, gridded reanalysis fields or satellite products; the variables may be of different physical types and need not lie on a regular grid. Each sample is augmented with a window of preceding samples (delay-coordinate embedding), since a segment of the recent history determines the underlying state more fully than a single snapshot does. A kernel function assigns a measure of similarity to each pair of embedded samples and, after normalization, defines a Markov smoothing operator on the data. The leading eigenvectors of the associated kernel matrix provide an orthonormal basis of functions adapted to the geometry of the data; for a linear (covariance) kernel they reduce to the principal components of empirical orthogonal function (EOF) analysis. In this basis the Koopman operator is represented by a finite matrix whose entries are time-lagged correlations of the basis functions. Eigenvectors of that matrix give approximate Koopman eigenfunctions, and its application to an observable or to a probability density gives forecasts. Forecasts are verified on data withheld from training, against reference forecasts such as persistence and climatology.

Pages in this section

The remaining pages treat these steps in turn. Koopman operator theory recalls the operator-theoretic description of dynamical systems and the role of eigenfunctions as indices of variability. Data-driven methods describes how the operators are approximated from time series: delay embeddings, kernels, nonlinear Laplacian spectral analysis (NLSA), Koopman spectral analysis and vector-valued spectral analysis. Kernel analog forecasting (KAF) is concerned with the estimation of conditional expectations, and hence of forecasts and of threshold-exceedance probabilities, from kernel-weighted historical analogs. Quantum mechanical data assimilation (QMDA) is concerned with the sequential evolution and updating of full forecast distributions.

General properties

No physical parameterization and no computational mesh enter the construction; the only inputs are the time series and the choice of kernel. Koopman eigenfunctions have a definite frequency and phase, so a forecast can be related to the state of an identified mode of variability. The same operators yield expected values, probabilities of exceeding a threshold, and full forecast distributions. Training is carried out once, after which the cost of a forecast is that of evaluating the kernel between the current observations and the training data. The estimators converge in the limit of large data, and their dependence on observational noise and on the choice of observed variables has been analysed (Giannakis 2019; Das et al. 2021).

Software

The methods are implemented in the open-source NLSA code, which comprises a Python toolkit with GPU support and the original MATLAB research code (Giannakis et al. 2026). The code is available from its GitHub repository, with accompanying documentation.

Where to start reading

An introduction to the ideas that link data science and dynamical systems is given by Berry et al. (2020). Koopman operator theory and its applications are reviewed by Brunton et al. (2022). A more mathematical account of the associated numerical methods, in particular of the variants of dynamic mode decomposition and their convergence properties, is given by Colbrook (2024). An application of the framework to climate dynamics is described by Froyland et al. (2021).
